Our innovation

A surface that slowly grows stone from the air around it.

In caves, flowstones form over thousands of years as mineral layers build up on rock. Stalactites and stalagmites are the best-known examples.

Our engineered flowstones take that idea onto existing buildings and other urban surfaces. They draw on ambient air, capture CO₂, and store it as solid carbonate that can be harvested and put to use.

Because they mount on surfaces cities already have, flowstones can be spread across many buildings instead of concentrated at one site. The bigger idea: cities that act as carbon sinks and as sources of sustainable materials.

  1. In

    Ambient air

    The installation works with the air already moving around a building.

  2. On

    Existing infrastructure

    Flowstones mount on walls and surfaces that are already there.

  3. Out

    Solid carbonate

    Captured CO₂ is stored as a solid material with a range of end uses.

Example end uses for harvested carbonate

  • Clinker substitute in cement
  • Aggregate
  • Soil stabilization

Who is this for

Spaces we're exploring.

We're in customer discovery, learning where flowstones fit best. These are some of the spaces we're looking at.

Buildings

Offices, campuses, lobbies and other spaces people use every day. Air quality and comfort are potential co-benefits we are studying.

Transportation

Highways, tunnels, airports, and subway and train stations: large surfaces that are already part of city life.

Education

Classrooms, museums and other learning spaces where people can watch carbon capture happen over time.

Materials

Carbonate as a material, with possible uses from cement and aggregate to soil stabilization.

Global North · Global South

Cities are on different emissions paths.

Since 2005, total CO₂ emissions have fallen in New York and London and risen in the five African cities shown here. Per person, most of those African cities still emit a small fraction of what New York does.

Global North

Add capture to the buildings already there.

In cities like New York and London, emissions are falling, and much of the building stock is already in place. Flowstones are designed for existing infrastructure, so they can be added to buildings that are already standing.

Global South

Build capture in as cities grow.

In fast-growing cities like Cairo, Lagos and Nairobi, emissions and construction are both rising. Flowstones could be part of new buildings from the start, and the carbonate they produce could serve local needs, from cement and aggregate to soil stabilization.

We are exploring both contexts. Our pilots so far are in New York City.

FAQ

Common questions.

Where can I see a flowstone?

Our SolarOne pilot at Stuyvesant Cove Park is running now. A pilot at Columbia's Mudd Building is coming next. See Visit us for details.

What happens to the carbonate?

We harvest it. At Governors Island, we cast harvested carbonate into a cement cylinder and 3D-printed small pieces that visitors took home. End uses include a clinker substitute in cement, aggregate and soil stabilization.

How much CO₂ does a flowstone capture?

We are measuring this through our pilots and a life cycle assessment now under way. We will share figures, labeled as estimated, once they are validated.

Can a flowstone go on my building or site?

We are in customer discovery, talking with people who manage buildings, transportation infrastructure, learning spaces and materials. Book a call to tell us about your space.

Does it improve air quality or save energy?

Air quality and comfort are potential co-benefits we are studying, including with CO₂ air-quality sensing at SolarOne. We do not claim energy savings.

Can my school get one?

We are exploring ways to bring flowstones into classrooms and other learning spaces. In October 2026 we ran an educator workshop with SolarOne. Get in touch if you would like to be involved.

Is the technology protected?

Yes. A provisional patent has been filed through Columbia Technology Ventures.
